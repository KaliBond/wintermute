When Institutions Crack, Trust Follows — Two to Three Years Later

US Legitimacy Crisis × CAMS Stress: a 30-year panel test, 1996–2025

Study · Kari Freyr McKern · July 2026 · Gallup Institutional Confidence & Pew Federal Government Trust cross-referenced with the CAMS structural panel for the United States · Companion: Study 1 — Interest Burden

When institutions start cracking internally, people stop trusting them — and CAMS picks up the crack about two to three years before the public does.

That's the short version. Here's the fuller picture.

We matched 30 years of Gallup and Pew survey data on public trust in American institutions against the CAMS structural scores for the US over the same period. Gallup asks people every year how much confidence they have in things like the military, banks, Congress, and the medical system. Pew asks a narrower question: do you trust the federal government to do the right thing? Both series run from the mid-1990s to 2025.

The finding is that when CAMS records rising institutional stress — meaning the underlying coordination machinery of the society is grinding harder, with the institutional-memory node (Archive) and the executive-governance node (Helm) degrading — the public trust numbers follow downward roughly two to three years later. The correlation gets stronger the further forward you look, which is what you'd expect from a genuine leading indicator rather than a coincidence.

This contrasts with the first study, on interest payments and debt. There, the fiscal burden built up first and CAMS tracked how well the system absorbed it — external pressure shaped the institutions. Here it's the other way around: internal structural degradation precedes the visible collapse in public confidence.

The practical implication is uncomfortable. The 2020 CAMS stress spike was the highest in the 30-year dataset. Two to three years later, Gallup hit its lowest reading since the 1970s and Pew hit a record low. The 2025 readings show the Archive node at its second-lowest value in the entire panel. The model predicts continued pressure through 2026–2028 — and that's before accounting for Pew trust already sitting at 9%, a 67-year low.

The structural diagnosis isn't about political turbulence on the surface. It's about whether the institutions that encode learning, sustain governance, and give citizens reason to believe the system will function tomorrow are actually doing that work — or burning through their reserves.

Headline

Hypothesis confirmed: CAMS Stress leads legitimacy collapse by 2–3 years — the exact opposite of the fiscal (Study 1) finding. Mean Stress vs. Gallup confidence strengthens from r = −0.41* at lag 0 to r = −0.69* at lag +3. In out-of-sample prediction, adding Mean Stress improves Gallup RMSE by −0.222 (−7.9%); Archive Stress is the best single predictor for Pew trust (−0.782 RMSE, −9.6%).

1. What was tested

Same three-part design as Study 1, run against a different target: Gallup's average institutional confidence and Pew's federal government trust, both against the annual CAMS panel for the US (1996–2025, 30 year-observations).

  1. Contemporaneous Pearson correlations between CAMS variables and each trust series
  2. Lead-lag analysis (lags −3 to +3): does CAMS stress lead legitimacy collapse, or follow it?
  3. AR(1) baseline vs. CAMS-augmented forecasting models, walk-forward out-of-sample (train ≤2020, test 2021–2025)

Archive Node Value and Helm Node Value are the strongest contemporaneous correlates of trust in both series (r ≈ +0.61 for both) — the institutional-memory node and the executive-governance node together explain most of the variance. That's structural, not a one-off blip.

2. The lead-lag profile

Positive lag means CAMS at year t predicts the trust reading at t + lag — i.e. CAMS leads. Every primary CAMS variable gets stronger, not weaker, as the lag increases toward +3, which is the signature of a genuine leading indicator.

4. The asymmetric mechanism

Fiscal burden is something US institutions absorb — Bond Strength tracks whether the system can keep carrying the load, so in Study 1 the fiscal series led CAMS. Political legitimacy runs the other way: CAMS structural degradation causes the trust collapse. Archive and Helm erosion precedes the drop in Gallup and Pew readings by 2–3 years.

Bond Strength shows a strong positive lead in both studies — it's the one variable that behaves the same way regardless of which mechanism is driving the system, which is itself informative: coordination capacity is the common substrate underneath both a fiscal-absorption story and a legitimacy-erosion story.

5. Where this leaves 2026–2028

The 2020 CAMS Stress spike (9.28, the highest in the 30-year panel) preceded the 2022–2023 trust trough (Gallup 27–26%, Pew 20–16%) by 2–3 years. The 2025 readings replicate that structural signature: Archive Node Value = 1.5 (the lowest in the panel outside 2020's 1.8), Archive Stress = 9.0, Mean Stress = 7.9. Pew trust has already fallen to 9% — a 67-year record low.

Read through the 3-year-lag model, the 2022–2024 structural readings are what's surfacing in the trust numbers now, and the 2025 readings are the trough being set for 2026–2028. The Pew collapse to 9% is the downstream expression of structural conditions already several years old, not a standalone political event.

6. Verdict

The oligarchic-capture hypothesis passes its first critical falsification test. CAMS Stress leads legitimacy decline by 2–3 years; the mechanism runs through the legitimacy channel, not the fiscal one. The 2025 readings replicate the structural signature of 2020, and the 3-year-lag model implies continued legitimacy pressure through 2026–2028.
