# The Meta-Animal and Its Nodes

## Situating CAMS in an Evolutionary Landscape

*Kari McKern / ComplexityWorkz — September 2026 (revised draft)*

***

There is a question so unfashionable that raising it at a dinner party, a faculty meeting or a funding panel earns a particular kind of silence: to what extent is a human society an animal group? I do not mean a metaphorical animal group, or "we are like wolves" on a motivational poster. I mean the literal proposition that a nation state, an empire, a corporation or a federation is the same class of object as a dolphin pod, a chimpanzee community or an elephant herd. On this view each is a bonded social unit held together by ancient machinery that long predates the species in question, and may therefore be studied in the same terms and scored on the same vitals.

The proposition is unfashionable for reasons I understand and partly share. It has a pedigree, and the pedigree is compromised. Herbert Spencer's "social organism" was the nineteenth century's version of this idea. It travelled quickly into social Darwinism, eugenics and the racial taxonomies of empire, and it did harm that is still being measured. The legitimate reflex of the twentieth century was to quarantine social explanation from biological explanation: culture here, genes there, and a fence between them patrolled by anthropologists on one side and biologists on the other. Anyone who climbs the fence today is suspected at once of heading back towards Spencer.

So I should say at the outset where this essay parts company with him. Spencer's organism was hierarchical and normative. It ranked societies from lower to higher and read moral lessons off the ranking. The meta-animal proposed here is functional and descriptive. It asks what any bonded social unit must do to persist, and it scores how well a given society is doing those things at a given time. It ranks no peoples. A score is a state, not a grade.

The quarantine was understandable. In the long run it was also a mistake, because the fence did not keep out bad biology. It kept out good self-knowledge. We now live with the consequences of having organised our self-understanding around abstractions that float free of any substrate at all.

## The age of competing abstractions

Look at what passes for social knowledge in 2026. We have economic models in which agents are frictionless optimisers and societies are equilibrium systems. We have ideological frameworks in which history is a tribunal and classes, civilisations or identity groups are its litigants. We have machine-learning systems that can simulate a society's conversation without possessing any model of what a society is. And we have dashboards, indices, composite scores and league tables: governance indicators, happiness reports, fragility rankings, democracy gradings. Each is a small act of abstraction. Each polls a few surfaces of social life and reports back a number that policymakers treat as a property of the thing itself.

None of these is contemptible. All of them are partial. What they share, and what I want to press on here, is that none asks the prior question: *what kind of thing is the entity being measured?*

* An equilibrium model assumes a market mechanism and asks whether it clears.

* A fragility index assumes institutional stress is measurable from above.

* A large language model assumes, insofar as it assumes anything, that the text of a society is a fair sample of the society.

That last assumption will return to trouble this essay, because it troubles every instrument that reads the past through documents, CAMS included.

The abstractions compete for the scarce resource of attention, and the competition is fierce because the abstractions are cheap. What costs something is the one perspective that has dropped out of the contest entirely. That is the evolutionary perspective, which says that before we score a society we should know what a society is. And what a society is has been under continuous development for a very long time.

This essay is a brief for that perspective, and for an instrument built to operate within it. The instrument is CAMS, the Complex Adaptive Model of Societies. It scores human societies on eight functional nodes (Helm, Shield, Lore, Stewards, Craft, Hands, Archive, Flow) across four vitals (Coherence, Capacity, Stress, Abstraction). What follows situates the instrument in the landscape it was drawn from: the deep behavioural record of social animals, including the social animal that builds instruments.

## The ditch and its bridges

Carl Sagan and Ann Druyan's *Shadows of Forgotten Ancestors* (1992) remains the most literate single survey of the evidence. It is a tour of the "protective ditches", the claimed discontinuities between human social behaviour and that of other animals, and of how each was bridged by twentieth-century field observation. Three decades on, the pattern it describes has held, though several of its bridges have been rebuilt in different places.

**Ethics.** The macaque experiment Sagan and Druyan made famous was reported by Masserman, Wechkin and Terris in 1964. Rhesus monkeys whose food came only by delivering a shock to a visible companion often chose hunger instead, and one went without food for twelve days. Refusal was more likely in animals that had themselves been shocked, and sight of the other monkey was enough to produce it. The finding should be held with care. Research ethics have tightened since, and the study has never been replicated in primates. It is a striking bridge, but a single-span one.

**Medicine.** Here the book's example has been superseded, and the replacement is better. Wrangham's early reading of Aspilia leaf-swallowing assumed a drug. That assumption drove more than twenty years of phytochemical searching, but by the 1990s chimpanzees had been seen swallowing leaves of nineteen species with few useful chemicals in common. Huffman resolved the puzzle mechanically. The proposed nematocide was absent from the swallowed leaves, and live worms were recovered from dung, trapped in the folded leaves and caught on their bristles. The rough leaves, swallowed on an empty stomach, irritate the gut and speed transit, scouring out parasites. Chemical self-medication exists as well. Huffman and colleagues showed that chimpanzees regularly eat the bitter stems of *Vernonia amygdalina*, which have anti-parasitic properties, and which Ugandan healers use for malaria, fever and intestinal parasites.

The most striking bridge is recent. In 2024 researchers reported a wild male Sumatran orangutan applying chewed leaves of Akar Kuning (*Fibraurea tinctoria*), a traditional medicinal climber with antibacterial, anti-inflammatory and analgesic activity, to a wound on his cheek. It was the first report of a wild animal treating a wound with a plant of known medicinal properties. Its authors suggest that medical wound treatment may date to a common ancestor of humans and orangutans.

**Culture.** The canonical case of Imo, the Koshima macaque who began washing sweet potatoes in 1953, still stands as culture. But later analysts, notably Galef, argued that the practice spread more slowly than the legend suggests and was shaped by human provisioning. The stronger evidence for animal culture now comes from elsewhere: regional chimpanzee traditions in tool use and grooming, and, as we shall see, whale dialects.

One may quarrel with individual bridges. Some of the book's figures are of its time, and its gene-sharing claim sits above the modern consensus range. But the cumulative argument does not depend on any single bridge. It depends on the pattern: every time a ditch was inspected closely, it narrowed. The differences that remain are differences of degree, and one difference of kind. As I will argue, that difference is narrower and more interesting than the one the philosophers expected.

I want to be precise about what this licenses. It does not license the inference that human societies are "nothing but" animal groups. Nor does it license any normative claim of the Spencerian type. What it licenses is a modelling decision: the human sybond belongs to the class of bonded animal social units, inherits that class's machinery, and its remarkable properties are best understood as extensions of that machinery rather than replacements for it.
## The sybond as meta-animal

I use the term *sybond* for the social bond scaled to the level of a society. It is the web of obligations, recognitions, shared symbols and coordinated routines that holds a human group together across numbers far beyond what a primate brain evolved to track. A baboon troop runs on personal recognition: every member known to every member, rank negotiated daily, alliances shifting in view of all. That machinery tops out at a few dozen individuals. The human version runs higher, around the hundred and fifty stable relationships Robin Dunbar associates with our neocortex, but it still tops out. Human societies run to millions. The puzzle is not that we are social; that is the old inheritance. The puzzle is that we are social at scale.

The resolution has a name in the literature, and this essay should have used it from the start. Mark Moffett distinguishes individual-recognition societies, in which every member must know every other, from anonymous societies, in which members need not know each other and are told apart from outsiders by shared features or behaviours. Ants such as the Argentine ant mark membership by scent, which allows anonymous societies numbering in the millions. Humans sit between chimpanzees and ants: we keep intimate ties with a limited circle, and we also carry societal markers that let us live peaceably among strangers. Moffett notes that those markers cut both ways, enabling peace within while predisposing us to suspicion of outsiders.

The sybond is Moffett's anonymous society seen from the inside, as a set of working organs. A flag, a treasury, a law code, a calendar, a national epic, a currency, a census: these are prosthetic bonding devices. They let strangers who will never meet coordinate as if they groomed together daily. Each has an evolutionary shadow:

* The flag is the troop's boundary call.
* The treasury is the shared cache.
* The law code is the submission gesture made portable.
* The epic is the identity chant at fireside scale.
* The currency is reciprocal altruism with the memory externalised.
* The census is individual recognition delegated to a record.

This differs from Yuval Harari's "imagined orders" in one important respect. Harari treats such devices as shared fictions and stops there. CAMS treats them as functional organs with evolutionary precursors and measurable states. A currency is not merely believed in. It can be more or less coherent, more or less capable, more or less stressed.

It also differs from any claim that marker-based bonding is uniquely human, because the comparative record no longer supports that claim. Analysing 23,429 codas recorded at 23 Pacific locations, Hersh and colleagues found that certain sperm whale click patterns behave like symbolic markers of clan membership. Where clan ranges overlap, the clans' identity codas become less alike, as if under pressure to sharpen the boundary. The authors call these arbitrary traits resembling human ethnic markers, and note how closely whale clan distributions resemble those of human ethnolinguistic groups. Symbolic bonding of strangers is therefore not the human difference. It is one more bridge.

This is why the sybond should be examined in the same terms as the dolphin pod or the elephant herd. The comparison is not meant to flatter or reduce. It is diagnostic. When we score a pod we ask how coherent it is, how capable, how stressed, how alert to its environment. When we score a troop we ask whether the rank order is settled or contested, whether the boundary is patrolled, whether the young are provisioned. These remain the right questions at a scale of millions. What changes is the instrumentation, not the anatomy.

## What CAMS is for

CAMS supplies that instrumentation. Its premise is modest: any society, at any time, must perform a recurring set of functions to persist, and these functions are old enough to have deep roots and distinct enough to be assessed separately. The eight nodes are the functions:

* **Helm** is decision and rank: who decides, how succession works, and whether rank is settled or contested.
* **Shield** is security: how the group manages threat, boundary and organised violence.
* **Lore** is meaning: the shared narratives a society tells itself about what it is.
* **Stewards** is provisioning: managing the flows that keep the population alive.
* **Craft** is technique: command over energy, materials and method.
* **Hands** is execution: the labour and logistics by which decisions become acts.
* **Archive** is record: what the society remembers, how faithfully, and who controls the remembering.
* **Flow** is exchange: the movement of people, goods, information and trust across the group's boundaries, including the exits by which pressure escapes.

The four vitals are the axes along which each node is read. Coherence asks whether the node's parts pull together. Capacity asks what it can currently do. Stress asks what load it bears relative to its means of relief. Abstraction asks how far the node has been lifted from face-to-face immediacy into symbol, record and model.

Two design commitments need explicit defence.

**The first is that CAMS polls history rather than theory.** A society's node states are recovered from the documentary and material record, and scored on calibrated scales. The record includes decisions taken, wars fought, prices fixed, techniques diffused, records kept and people moved. This is slower and less elegant than deriving states from an equilibrium model. But the entity being measured is the entity that existed.

Honesty requires two qualifications.

The eight-node taxonomy is itself a theory, a functional hypothesis about what societies must do. "Polling history rather than theory" therefore means that the node states come from the record, while the node frame is proposed in advance and must earn its keep empirically.

The polling is also mediated. CAMS scores are produced by multiple independent scorers, currently language models working from the historical literature in separate contexts. Their outputs are aggregated, and where the evidence is thin they are required to report NA rather than guess. That is a real safeguard, but it does not escape the problem this essay raised against the competing abstractions. A scorer that reads history reads the text of history, and the text over-represents some societies, periods and viewpoints. The Anglophone historiography of Russia and China is an obvious case. CAMS's defence is not that it avoids this bias but that it exposes it. The NA rate per society-period is itself reported, and an opaque record shows up as missingness, not as confident numbers.

The dataset now spans thirty-six societies and more than thirty thousand society-year observations. Two validation results should be described for what they are:

* A zero-error formalism pass across the full panel confirms that the mathematics is applied consistently. It is a check on internal consistency, not on validity.
* A rank-concordance check against an independent European state-capacity series — the German ENS series, 1880–2026 — returns Spearman ρ = 0.9552. That is encouraging. But some of the concordance may reflect shared sources, since scorers and series builders read overlapping historiography. The next step is validation against series constructed from primary quantitative data the scorers never see, such as fiscal records, census counts and conflict datasets.

**The second commitment is uniform scales.** Rome and Song China are scored on identical rubrics; the Habsburg monarchy and post-war Japan are read with the same instruments. If the sybond is one kind of thing, uniform scoring is basic hygiene, for the same reason one uses the same thermometer in every ward. This does not require dismissing interpretive anthropology. Local knowledge belongs in the evidence, not in the rubric. What counts as a Lore crisis in Tang China or Tokugawa Japan must be learned from those societies' own terms. The scale on which that crisis is registered must be common, or comparison, and with it measurement, becomes impossible. One ruler, many informants.

## Reading the nodes in deep time

Four mappings are direct enough to state plainly. The comparative evidence for them is strong, and in each case it changes how the node should be scored.

### Helm: hierarchy, and the coalition against it

Sagan and Druyan's eleventh chapter is the best short monograph on the dominance hierarchy. It is ancient and, surprisingly, violence-reducing. When rank is unambiguous, conflict is ritualised and rare, and violence concentrates where rank is contested. The alpha's authority rests on a trade: deference in exchange for protection, adjudication and reassurance. De Waal's *Chimpanzee Politics* (1982) adds the decisive nuance. Where alliances can form, power is brokered and the group buys stability at the price of friction.

The earlier draft of this essay said humans added the means of checking the alpha. That is wrong, or at least late. Christopher Boehm's *Hierarchy in the Forest* (1999) shows the check is far older than parliaments. Boehm argues that we share a tendency toward despotism with chimpanzees and bonobos, yet nomadic foragers have been consistently egalitarian, perhaps for a very long time. His explanation is the "reverse dominance hierarchy": adult males combine to stop any one of them, alone or with a few allies, from dominating the rest. So what looks like natural egalitarianism is an inverted dominance order, the majority vigilantly suppressing would-be masters, by assassination if necessary. Boehm also notes that this male egalitarianism did not necessarily extend to women. That caution belongs in any honest account.

The mechanics are graded. The first response to a boaster or non-sharer is teasing; if that fails, the band shuns him, treating him as absent. The offender either reforms or leaves to join another band.

That final step matters for CAMS. The foragers' ultimate check on dominance is exit, which means the Helm and Flow nodes are coupled at the root. The deep human pattern is therefore not simply chimpanzee hierarchy scaled up. It is closer to a U-shape: despotic ape ancestry, egalitarian foragers, then re-emerging despotism once agriculture and storable surplus made exit costly and hoarding possible. Boehm observes that early kingdoms and many modern states are more despotic than any ape.

For scoring, Helm Coherence cannot measure only how settled rank is. It must also read the state of the counter-dominance machinery: the coalitions, courts, councils, assemblies and exits by which the ranked check the rankers. A Helm that is settled because the counter-coalition has been dismantled is not healthy. It is quiet. The rule also cuts in the other direction. The form of counter-dominance varies by civilisation. It may be a party's collective leadership norms, a Confucian remonstrance tradition, a parliament or a village assembly. Reading its presence or absence from the evidence, rather than from a preferred constitutional template, is what keeps the node from becoming a disguised democracy index.

### Shield: the boundary, and the lethality line

The Shield's deep record is uncomfortable. Chimpanzee communities patrol borders and raid neighbours. Over the course of Goodall's Gombe study, one community destroyed a splinter group male by male (Goodall 1986). Sagan and Druyan render this in street vernacular, "We don't like Strangers. Our guys see Strangers, they go crazy". The effect is unsettling because in-group tenderness and out-group ferocity appear in the same individuals within minutes.

The bonobo, long cast as our peaceable counter-image, has been substantially redrawn. In a 2024 field comparison, Mouginot and colleagues found male bonobos aggressive more often than chimpanzees: 2.8 times as many aggressive interactions and three times as many physical ones. Male bonobo aggression was directed almost entirely at other males, whereas chimpanzees more often targeted females. In both species the more aggressive males mated more. Yet the old contrast survives on one axis. Bonobos have never been seen using lethal force on one another. Severe and lethal male aggression, within and between communities, is common in chimpanzees but absent in bonobos.

This sharpens the Shield node rather than blurring it. Frequency of aggression and lethality of aggression are different variables, and they do not move together. Within-species aggression is overwhelmingly display and posture, which the bonobo data show in abundance, and it is distinct from the coalitionary killing of outsiders. A Shield score that conflates rattling sabres with exterminating neighbours misreads both. It will over-alarm about noisy but contained rivalries and under-alarm about quiet preparation for lethal campaigns. CAMS should score posture and lethality separately, and should treat the transition from one to the other as the event of interest.

### Stress: load against relief

John Calhoun's rodent enclosures became the twentieth century's parable of crowding. His rats were given everything except space; the population boomed and then suffered such severe behavioural disruption that it died out. The lesson drawn, for rats and by extension humans, was that crowding breeds pathology. Sagan and Druyan pointed out what the parable omits. Under comparable crowding, vervet monkeys begin avoiding one another, and chimpanzees increase peacemaking through grooming and conciliation. "Are we not more like chimps than like rats?" The caged rats could not leave.

Human research has since borne this out. In Jonathan Freedman's influential experiments, people working under varying densities showed few pathologies. Attention shifted to the factors that mediate density, helped by a distinction between density as a physical measure and crowding as a subjective response. Whether people felt crowded depended on privacy needs, social role and, above all, their ability to control the situation. Patricia Draper's study of !Kung camps (1973) made the same point from the field: close living does not by itself produce the Calhoun signature where people remain free to move.

The finding converges with Boehm's. In both, the decisive variable is exit and control, not load. For CAMS this settles how Stress is scored. It is never raw pressure, always pressure relative to the society's appeasement and exit machinery. It also makes Flow a pressure-relief organ rather than a convenience. Block the exits of any sybond, whether emigration, trade, the circulation of dissent or the movement of labour, and it begins to show the Calhoun signature.

The claim is testable, and I have tested it against the panel: thirty-six societies, 4,047 society-years, Stress and Flow Capacity read at year t, subsequent Coherence change over one- and five-year horizons, society fixed effects, standard errors clustered by society. The result refuses the flattering version of the story, and I should report it exactly as it came out. High-Stress society-years are on average followed by Coherence *gains*, not losses — the panel mean-reverts at annual scale, stress spikes get scored as resolved, and the carry-forward in the scoring imposes some oscillation of its own. The hypothesis as I first wrote it, that stress produces subsequent loss which Flow then cushions, is wrong in sign and cannot be claimed.

What survives is subtler and, I think, more interesting. The interaction term is negative and significant at the one-year horizon (p = 0.007 pooled, 0.019 with fixed effects): Flow Capacity roughly halves the coupling between Stress and subsequent Coherence movement, and at the 75th percentile of Flow Capacity the coupling falls to nearly zero. Because the scoring carries scores forward where the record is thin, I audited the panel for repeats: fifteen of the thirty-six societies carry the previous year's scores on more than thirty per cent of society-years, led by Israel (87.5 per cent), Lebanon (86.7) and Indonesia (85.9). Dropping every carry-forward repeat leaves 2,847 of the 4,047 society-years, and on that de-smoothed panel the decoupling does not weaken; it sharpens (interaction β ≈ −0.013, p = 0.0001 pooled, 0.0006 with fixed effects; coupling at the 25th percentile of Flow Capacity β ≈ 0.033, at the 75th β ≈ 0.003). Read out plainly: where the exits are open, stress leaves almost no footprint in the coherence record; where they are blocked, the same load propagates into visible institutional movement. That is the Calhoun signature in panel form — not that crowded cages raise the death rate, but that a caged sybond transmits every jolt, while a sybond with open flows absorbs them.

The cleaner confirmation comes from volatility rather than direction. Stress raises the *magnitude* of subsequent Coherence swings, up or down, by a robust margin across every specification; Flow Capacity damps that turbulence, significantly so at the five-year horizon (p = 0.010 with fixed effects on the full panel, 0.0003 on the de-smoothed panel). An instrument built to poll history has now returned its first evolutionary-derived prediction, in the direction the comparative record predicted, with the honest caveat that the scores are partly smoothed — which is why the carry-forward audit and the de-smoothed replication matter — that the associations are observational, and that reverse paths remain plausible. An origin story has become a quantitative claim about the data, and the data have answered.

### Archive and Craft: the ratchet

Here, finally, is the difference of kind, now stated more narrowly than before. Other animals transmit culture. Other animals, as the sperm whales show, mark group identity with arbitrary symbols. What no other animal does is compound the inheritance, at scale, across generations of strangers. Michael Tomasello calls this the "ratchet effect", in which each innovation is preserved faithfully enough to become the platform for the next. Joseph Henrich's work on cumulative culture shows how the ratchet, once turning, makes human groups dependent on collective knowledge no individual could reinvent.

Human Archive externalises memory from a brain that tops out at around a hundred and fifty relationships. Human Craft externalises technique from imitation of elders. The result compounds: each generation starts from a stock its ancestors could not have held in their heads. This is the meta-animal's exoskeleton. Abstraction, the fourth vital, scores how far a society has lifted its functions onto it.

Societies differ enormously on this axis. Some keep census, law and chronicle; some keep only the epic and the tax roll; some keep almost nothing, and must be reconstructed from archaeology. This last point loops back to method. The societies with the thinnest Archive are exactly those for which CAMS's text-mediated scorers will return the most NA. The instrument's blind spots are therefore predictable from one of its own variables. I count that as a strength, provided it is reported.

The remaining nodes, Lore, Stewards and Hands, have their shadows too: the identity chant, the shared cache, the hunt at a million-man scale. But the four above are where the comparative evidence is strongest, and I will not claim more certainty than the record gives.

## The objections, taken seriously

**Determinism.** If our institutions express ancient algorithms, are we excused, or doomed? The historical record answers this. Hereditary rule, chattel slavery and the formal subordination of women were each defended for millennia as natural and divinely ordained. Each has been delegitimised, not everywhere and not completely, within a historical span far too short for genetic change. Culture draws on a wide inherited repertoire, and that repertoire includes the predispositions for restraint, coalition and fairness that Boehm's foragers enforced. If anything the evolutionary view increases responsibility. We cannot blame our natures for institutions we have demonstrably discarded.

**The is–ought slide.** Reading norms off nature is a fallacy whichever direction it runs, and the evolutionary framing is a better prophylactic against it than the quarantine was, because it lets us see the machinery we are refusing to rationalise. Xenophobia has a real adaptive history; Moffett's point is that the markers which let strangers live together are the same markers that make outsiders suspect. That is a fact about the past, not a recommendation. The same framework shows the algorithm can be evoked by demagogues and damped by design. Knowing the shadow is the precondition of not being governed by it.

This has a contemporary edge. An instrument that scores every society on the same scales, from the same kind of evidence, is one of the few available defences against the inherited reflex to read the rival troop's every move as menace. When China or Russia is scored by the rubric applied to Britain or the United States, the result may be flattering or damning. Either way it is a measurement, not a reflex. In an era of hardening blocs, the common interest of every sybond lies in reading each other's states accurately.

**The bonobo caution, revised.** Chimpanzees and bonobos are both our close kin, and their social arrangements differ markedly. The difference turns out to be subtler than peace against war: bonobo males fight often but do not kill, and bonobo females form coalitions that constrain male coercion. A shared deep repertoire did not fix the outcome. Ecology and history selected from the menu, and the variables that separate the two species are the shape of coalitions and the ceiling on lethality, not the presence of aggression. So it is with human societies. The evolutionary landscape constrains the attractors; it does not fix the trajectory. For CAMS this is the whole point. Node states are state variables of an attractor landscape, scored per society per period, never essences of peoples. A score is a photograph of a chronic negotiation, not a verdict on a civilisation.

## Against the age of abstractions

I return to where the argument began: an age in which societies are equilibrium systems, historical litigants, training distributions or league-table entries, and in which the question *what kind of thing is a society?* is hardly ever asked.

CAMS is an attempt to keep the question alive and give it an instrument. Its wager is that the human sybond is one more society among societies: an anonymous society of the kind Moffett describes, built on markers like those the whales use, checked by coalitions like those Boehm's foragers formed, and unique chiefly in its ratchet. It can therefore be examined, scored and compared in the same terms as the rest of its class.

The wager forbids the hermeneutic alibi, the ideological shortcut and the model-driven inference. In exchange it buys three things:

* a stable unit of analysis that does not change when the theory changes;
* vitals legible across the whole recorded history of the species;
* a reminder, in every score and every NA, that the scored entity is an animal that inherited its machinery and must now, with whatever instruments it can build, learn to read its own shadows, including the shadows cast by the instruments themselves.

Sagan and Druyan end their book with a sentence I have never been able to shake. Maturity, they write, entails "a readiness, painful and wrenching though it may be, to look squarely into the long dark places, into the fearsome shadows," in the hope that ancestral remembrance may be "a light by which to see our children safely home" (1992, ch. 21). That is as good a statement of the purpose of measuring societies as I know. We are not a fallen animal or a risen one. We are an animal with an archive, and the archive is only useful if we are willing to read in it what we are.
