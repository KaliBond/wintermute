Organisational Pathologies

The ways groups go wrong look the same at every scale. Can they be recovered, by number, from the history of nations?

Kari McKern · ComplexityWorkz · 20 October 2026 · six frozen signatures · 37 societies from JUNO v3 · progress report

You already know these people. The boss who has stopped taking calls. The family that has quietly forgotten why a relative is not spoken of. The committee that runs on the story it tells itself while the cupboard goes bare. The household where the bloke in charge is plainly out of his depth and everyone else is holding the place together around him.

Nobody needs a theory to spot them. We are humans, and humans are very good at noticing when the group has gone wrong. The same wrongness, it seems, turns up at every scale. The kitchen table, the office, the cabinet room and the nation all fail in a handful of recognisable ways. These failures are outliers with a shape, and the shape is the same whether the group is five people or fifty million.

This is a progress report on recovering them from the history of nations.

The instrument

CAMS treats any society as a complex adaptive system performing eight functions:

Helmsteers
Shieldkeeps force and order
Loresupplies meaning
Stewardshold and allocate assets
Flowmoves things around
Craftmakes things that need skill
Handsdo the labour
Archiveremembers

Each function is scored each year for Coherence (C), Capacity (K), Stress (S) and Abstraction (A), and its node value is:

V = C + K + A/2 − S

Two layers sit underneath. Coordination problems explain why the functions exist at all. Anything that wants to outlast its founders has to steer, defend, remember and feed itself. The primate who runs it explains why the same failures keep coming back. We are prone to cheating, lying and status-seeking, and we carry the same tricks into every institution we build.

The topology is the stage. We supply the drama.

The scores are AI assessments, and we accept them as given, in good faith on the evidence available to the rater. The warrant is repeatability, in the same way climate historians dig temperature records out of monastic archives that were never kept for that purpose. We ask the same question of the same evidence over and over, and the answer either holds or it does not. Here it largely holds.

No scoring pass is ever asked about “Helm isolation” or “Archive amnesia”. The raters score functions, year by year. The signatures are patterns we look for afterwards, in how the scores change relative to one another.

The pathologies

Each signature is a rule written in the scores: a way of catching one of those familiar failures at national scale. A pathology is a relationship between functions, not just a low score. Two societies can have the same average and be arranged very differently.

The test is simple. If these are real outliers and not just the noise of bad years, they should cluster where history says something went wrong, and they should appear in societies that have nothing else in common.

What held

JUNO v3 holds 42 societies and 41,914 rows of scores. For the frozen run I used 37 of them. Denmark, India, Turkey, Thailand and Canada were excluded in advance, and Israel was used only from 2015.

Then the thresholds were frozen, so no rule could be nudged to catch the event I hoped to catch. Under those rules the signatures fired in 696 society-years. Each result below is compared against chance: every society’s firing years are slid round in time 10,000 times, keeping their number and spacing, to see how often they would land in the same places by accident.

They cluster where history says something went wrong

Archive Amnesia fired 15 times in its named windows against 0.75 expected by chance. That is twenty times the chance rate. Helm Isolation and Shield Inversion fire about twice as often as chance in theirs, and Helm Incompetence four times against 0.18.

The casebook

Of 18 named windows, 12 hold, and the list reads like a casebook of things people noticed at the time.

The six that dropped out: Laos 1975, Germany 1931 and Hong Kong 1997 (Archive Amnesia); Germany 1939 and Pakistan 1968–70 (Helm Incompetence); Iraq 2015–25 (Shield Inversion). Australia 1976–80 was a check window, not a claimed hit, and correctly does not fire.

They travel together

The pathologies also travel together, the way they do in families. Helm Isolation and Shield Inversion follow one another, in either order, in nine nations. Helm Incompetence leads to Helm Isolation in five. The United Kingdom goes from one to the other between 2016 and 2018; Venezuela between 2008 and 2009.

First the idiots arrive, then they stop taking calls. Anyone who has watched a workplace go sour will recognise the order. Other sequences show up too: the Narrative–Material Gap is followed by Late Abstraction Collapse in five nations, and Archive Amnesia by Shield Inversion in four.

I still owe these chains a base-rate comparison before I lean on them. Signatures that share a function can also fire together simply because they are built from the same scores.

One signature looks ahead

One signature does more than recognise. Late Abstraction Collapse comes before big falls (an annual drop of 2 or more in mean V within three years) 62 times, against 38.6 expected. Helm Incompetence and the Narrative–Material Gap lean the same way, at p = 0.074 and p = 0.072: close, but not yet over the bar. The rest mark episodes rather than forecast them.

The plainest result

The plainest result may be the most important. A few simple rules on eight functions catch events that humans already recognise. The scores know nothing of Brexit or Chávez. The rules found the shapes anyway.

The pathologies we sense in our own families and offices are real structures, and they can be recovered, by number, from the record of whole societies.

Not every outlier is a disease. A Helm that isolates itself in a siege may be doing the only thing that works, and a Shield that rises against a real external threat is doing its job. The signatures are diagnoses, and a doctor can name a fever without deciding it should never have happened.

What comes next

A prospective watchlist will name societies showing early signatures now, so the future can mark my homework instead of me marking it.

And the same frozen rules will be run unchanged below the nation, to recover quantitatively the structures we have already seen there. The first test (P4) scores Boeing, Theranos and WorldCom against matched firms with no known crisis, with the eight functions mapped onto the firm: Helm is the board and executive, Shield is compliance and legal, Archive is records and audit. Every firing is reported, including false alarms in the controls.

A recovery catalogue follows: if damaging configurations recur, recovery should too. Archive recovery, Helm recoupling, Shield restraint and the reconnection of Lore with the material functions will be defined, frozen and searched for with the same discipline.

If the numbers hold there too, we will have something better than another explanation of the last collapse after it happened. Anyone who has lived inside a failing family, firm or country has felt these patterns. CAMS shows they can be recovered from the numbers as well.

The lesson

These failures aren't fate. They are a small, familiar repertoire of human behaviours, so they can be named early. The family that names the silence can stop the forgetting. The workplace that notices the boss has stopped taking calls can route around them. A nation can do the same if it watches the right relationships: memory against force, the top against everyone else, the story against the cupboard.

The second lesson is for those in charge. Your failures will look ordinary, and they will show. The arrangement underneath shifts before the title is lost, and anyone who knows how to read it can see it shift.

Which societies, and why five were left out

The dataset (juno/v3/JUNO_Unified_Dataset_v3_2026-10-19.csv) holds 41,914 rows for 42 societies. Five whole series were excluded in advance because of measurement problems:

  • Denmark: Stress recorded as zero throughout.
  • India and Turkey: 26–31 sampled years each.
  • Thailand: 5-yearly scoring.
  • Canada: 33 years.

Israel stays in, but only from 2015, because the earlier series is flat. The draft-threshold counts in the Signature Pack (sig.py) had different coverage: they excluded only Denmark and pre-2015 Israel, and their annual-only rules skipped the non-annual series.

An exploratory finding: stress comes before lost capacity

Exploratory, 19 October, not preregistered. Across 38,704 node-years in 37 societies, a rise in Stress is followed the next year by a fall in Capacity (r = −0.072), but not the reverse (r = +0.004). The society-cluster bootstrap 95% CI of the difference is [−0.103, −0.052], and the asymmetry holds in 33 of 37 societies. V has no time order built in, so this is not an artefact of the formula.

  • B. Rule transfer (next test): the frozen national rules, unchanged, pick out the same pathologies in firms and institutions.
  • C. Calibrated transfer (later): thresholds and outcome rates carry across scales quantitatively.
